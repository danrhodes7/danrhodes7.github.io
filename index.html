Projects

Analysing London's Train Network

This project combines urban planning questions with a numerical approach, coded in Python. It had three parts.

1. Data analysis

I analysed population density, population per borough, transport density and the number of stations per borough to find areas with large populations but poor access to public transport. This showed where improvements and new connections are most needed.

2. Graph theory

I modelled the rail network as a graph to measure how well stations connect to each other. There are several metrics for this, including betweenness centrality and average path length. I focused on k-edge connectivity.

I implemented k-edge augmentation. In simple terms, the algorithm finds every dead-end line and connects it to other lines, so that every station has at least k separate routes to every other station. If one line goes down, there is always a second route. The new connections are chosen to keep their total length as short as possible.

I tested several scenarios, including adding the Superloop, changing the number of zones, and including proposed lines such as the West London Orbital and the DLR extension to Thamesmead. Some of the results are below.

k-edge augmentation results (new links needed, total new track length)
Scenario k = 2 k = 3
Zones 1-6 37 links, 114 km 298 links, 365 km
Zones 1-6 with Superloop 38 links, 104 km 297 links, 359 km
Zones 1-6 with proposed lines 37 links, 107 km 292 links, 355 km
Zones 1-6 with proposed lines and Superloop 36 links, 96 km 287 links, 345 km

Adding the Superloop and the proposed lines reduces the amount of new track needed to connect the dead ends.

3. Random and targeted station removal

I tested how well the network withstands failures, both before and after augmentation. Random removal simulates incidents such as line failures, fires or animals on the tracks. Targeted removal takes out the best-connected stations first, simulating events such as targeted strike action. The augmented networks performed significantly better under both.

Overall, the project shows that even small increases in connectivity can greatly improve a network's resilience.

Creating a Chess Engine for Fortress Positions

For my final year project at Imperial College London, I created a chess engine focused on fortress positions, where one side has a material advantage but cannot force a win. These positions are difficult for chess engines, which can easily miss a move such as a sacrifice that looks bad at first but gives an advantage in the long run.

The engine works in three stages:

1. Exhaustive search

The engine searches every possible line for the first few moves (3 to 7, adjustable in the interface). This ensures that no breakthrough move, such as a sacrifice or other counter-intuitive move, is missed, and that promising lines are not pruned away too early.

2. Further evaluation

From each position found, the engine runs an alpha-beta pruning search to find the most promising lines, and tracks how the evaluation changes as the search goes deeper. If the evaluation stays flat, a fortress is assumed. If it keeps rising, a breakthrough is assumed. These lines are then passed to the final stage.

3. Heuristic analysis

If a breakthrough is found, the engine uses heuristics adapted from Stockfish, such as material advantage, piece activity and tactics, to find a winning line. If not, it falls back on fortress heuristics that I wrote myself, and holds the draw.

The project was written entirely in C++. The report and poster below give more detail.
